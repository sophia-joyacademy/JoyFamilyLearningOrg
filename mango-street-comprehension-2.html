2. The author describes the garden as full of which of the following?

3. Why do Tito's friends take Sally's keys?

5. How does Sally react when Esperanza tries to rescue her?

6. After the incident with Sally, what does Esperanza do in the garden?

Part B — Open Response

7. At the beginning of the vignette, the author describes the garden as a magical, exciting place for the neighborhood children. By the end, what has the garden become for Esperanza? Use details from the text to support your answer.

Think about what happens to Esperanza's feelings about the garden after the Sally incident. What does she do there at the end? The author says she "didn't feel so good" — what does the garden represent at that point?

8. Esperanza runs to get an adult to help Sally, but her mother does not understand why she is upset. What does this tell us about the gap between what Esperanza sees and what the adults around her see?

Think about what Esperanza is trying to explain. Why might her mother not understand? What does this moment show about the difference between Esperanza's world and the adult world?

9. Sally is not upset about what happened with the boys — she is embarrassed by Esperanza. What does Sally's reaction tell us about the different ways the two girls understand what "growing up" means?

Sally chooses to stay with the boys. She doesn't want to be rescued. What does Sally want? What does Esperanza want for her? Why are they different?

10. The author titled this vignette "The Monkey Garden," but the monkey itself is only mentioned briefly. Why do you think the author chose this title? What does the garden represent in this vignette? Refer to what the author actually writes.

Think about what the garden used to be (the monkey's owner left) and what it becomes (overgrown, wild, a place where bad things happen). What kind of place is it for children at the start vs. the end?

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. Where are Esperanza and Sally when this vignette takes place?

2. What does Sally do that leaves Esperanza alone?

3. The author writes that Esperanza called out for Sally. What does this detail show about how Esperanza feels during the assault?

4. Esperanza says that what happened was "not what you said at all." Who is she talking to, and what had they told her?

5. The author addresses Sally directly throughout this vignette. What is the overall tone of Esperanza's words to Sally?

6. The red clowns are mentioned in the title and appear in the background of this vignette. What contrast do they create with what happens to Esperanza?

Part B — Open Response

7. Esperanza says the women and Sally told her stories about love and boys that turned out to be lies. What does this suggest about how girls in the book are prepared — or not prepared — for the real dangers they face?

Think about what stories Esperanza heard and what actually happened. The author is showing a gap between what girls are told and what they experience. What is dangerous about that gap?

8. The author writes this vignette in the second person — Esperanza speaks directly to Sally as "you." How does this choice of voice affect the reader? Why might the author have written it this way instead of just describing what happened?

Read the vignette again and notice every time Esperanza says "you" to Sally. What feeling does that create? Is it like an accusation, a confession, a cry for help? How would the vignette feel different if Esperanza just said "Sally wasn't there"?

9. This is one of the most painful vignettes in the book. The author does not describe the assault in direct detail — she uses indirect language. What effect does this have? Is it more powerful or less powerful than a direct description? Explain using specific words from the text.

The author writes things like "not what you said at all" and "I called your name." She never states exactly what happened. Think about why a writer might make this choice. What does the reader fill in?

10. "Red Clowns" and "The Monkey Garden" both involve Sally leaving Esperanza in a dangerous or painful situation. What does Sally represent for Esperanza across these two vignettes? Use evidence from both.

In both vignettes, where is Sally when Esperanza needs her? What did Esperanza expect from Sally? What did she actually get? Think about trust and friendship.

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. Who does Sally marry, according to the author?

2. When did Sally get married?

3. The author writes that Sally's husband "doesn't let her" do certain things. Which of the following does the text say he will not allow?

4. Why did Sally want to get married, according to the author?

5. The title "Linoleum Roses" refers to what Sally looks at in her new home. What does this image suggest about Sally's life?

6. How does the author describe Sally's feelings about her new life?

Part B — Open Response

7. Sally married to escape her father, but the author shows her in a marriage where her husband also controls her. In your own words, explain the trap the author is describing. Use specific details from the text.

Sally's father controlled her before marriage. Now her husband controls her after marriage. What changed? What stayed the same? What is the author showing about the options available to Sally?

8. The author lists the things in Sally's apartment — the pots and pans, the pictures on the wall, the floors. Why does the author spend time on these objects? What do they tell us about Sally's life?

Think about what Sally is allowed to do in her apartment. These objects — the things she looks at, counts, arranges — are her entire world now. What does that world look like? Is it enough?

9. Compare Sally's situation to Mama's situation in "A Smart Cookie." What do these two women have in common? What is different about their circumstances?

Think about what each woman wanted, what stopped her, and what her daily life looks like. Both are women who had potential — what happened to it?

10. The title uses the word "roses" — a symbol of beauty and love — but puts the word "linoleum" before it. Linoleum is a hard, cheap floor covering. What do you think the author is saying about Sally's marriage by combining these two words?

A real rose is alive and fragrant. A linoleum rose is flat, printed, and cannot grow. What does each kind of rose represent? What is the author saying about the difference between what Sally hoped for and what she got?

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. Why are the three sisters present in this vignette?

2. How does the author describe the three sisters — what is unusual about them?

3. One of the three sisters calls Esperanza aside. What does she tell Esperanza about her future?

4. The sister gives Esperanza a specific instruction about leaving. What is it?

5. The sister says: "You will always be Mango Street." What does Esperanza think of this at the time?

6. The three sisters smell of "roses and peppermint candy." What effect does this detail create?

Part B — Open Response

7. The three sisters seem to know things about Esperanza that she has not told them. What do you think the author wants us to understand about these women? Are they simply wise elders, or does the author give them a more mysterious role?

Think about how they are described — their age, their smell, how they look at Esperanza. What gifts do they seem to have? Why might the author make them seem magical rather than ordinary?

8. The sister tells Esperanza to "come back" for the ones who cannot leave. Who are "the ones who cannot leave"? Name at least two characters from earlier in the book who fit this description and explain why they cannot leave.

Think about the women in the book who are stuck — trapped by husbands, fathers, poverty, or circumstances. Mamacita, Sally, Rafaela, Minerva — what stops each of them from leaving?

9. This is the first time in the book that someone outside Esperanza's family tells her directly that she is destined for something. How does this moment feel different from other moments in the book when adults speak to Esperanza?

Think about how other adults in the book treat Esperanza — do they see her future? Do they encourage her? How is this moment different from, say, the moment with her mother in "A Smart Cookie"?

10. This vignette takes place at a funeral — a baby has died. Why do you think the author placed this important prophecy inside a scene of grief? What does the combination of death and promise mean?

A baby's death means an end — a life that never got to begin. But the three sisters give Esperanza a message about her future. What is the author saying about endings and beginnings? About what gets lost and what might survive?

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. What does Esperanza tell Alicia she does NOT have?

2. What is Alicia's response when Esperanza denies that Mango Street is her home?

3. Esperanza says she will not come back to Mango Street unless someone makes it better. Who does Alicia suggest might do that?

4. Alicia is described earlier in the book as a girl who goes to university and works hard. How does her situation make her an appropriate person to have this conversation with Esperanza?

5. The vignette is very short. The author ends it with Alicia's question hanging in the air without a clear answer. What effect does this create?

6. Earlier in the book, Esperanza says she is ashamed of where she lives. In this vignette, Alicia challenges that shame. What does Alicia seem to believe about one's responsibility to one's home?

Part B — Open Response

7. Esperanza says she doesn't have a house and doesn't want one on Mango Street. But is she telling the whole truth? Based on what you've read in the book, what does Esperanza actually want — and is that different from what she says here?

Think about the vignette "A House of My Own." What kind of house does Esperanza dream of? How is that different from rejecting Mango Street? Is it possible to want to leave AND still care about where you come from?

8. Alicia tells Esperanza that Mango Street is her home "whether she likes it or not." Do you agree with Alicia? Can a place be your home even if you are ashamed of it or want to escape it? Use details from the book to support your view.

Think about how the book began — Esperanza's shame about the house on Mango Street. But also think about the three sisters' words. What does it mean to "belong" to a place?

9. Alicia's final implied question — who will make Mango Street better if not Esperanza? — connects to what the three sisters told Esperanza. How do these two conversations together help us understand what the author wants Esperanza (and the reader) to feel responsible for?

The three sisters said "come back for those who cannot leave." Alicia says "who's going to make it better?" Are these the same message? What is the author saying about people who are able to escape — what do they owe to those left behind?

10. This vignette is only a few sentences long. Yet it carries enormous meaning. Why do you think the author made it so short? What is the effect of compressing this important conversation into such a brief space?

Think about what's said and what's NOT said. Short doesn't mean unimportant — in fact, the brevity might make it hit harder. What hangs in the silence after the last line?

A House of My Own

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. In this vignette, Esperanza describes the house she wants. She specifically says it is NOT whose house?

2. The author describes the house as "quiet as snow." What does this image suggest about what Esperanza wants from her house?

3. The author writes that the house should be "clean as paper before the poem." What does "before the poem" mean here?

4. This is one of the shortest vignettes in the book. What does the author seem to be saying by keeping it so brief and poetic?

5. The house Esperanza describes is "only mine." Why is the word "only" important here?

6. The author places this vignette near the end of the book, just before "Mango Says Goodbye Sometimes." Why is this an important position for this vignette?

Part B — Open Response

7. The house Esperanza wants is defined by what it is NOT: not a man's, not a daddy's. Why does the author define the house by its negative — what it isn't — rather than just describing what it is? What does this tell us about Esperanza's life so far?

Think about whose houses Esperanza has lived in and near throughout the book. Think about the women you've read about — Sally, Rafaela, Mamacita. What has defined all of their houses? What is Esperanza rejecting?

8. "Clean as paper before the poem." The author connects the idea of a house to the idea of writing. What does this suggest about what a house of her own means for Esperanza beyond just having a place to live?

A blank page is where stories and poems begin. If the house is "clean as paper before the poem," what can happen in it? What does having her own space allow Esperanza to do or become?

9. Think about the house on Mango Street that Esperanza has been ashamed of throughout the book, and compare it to the house she describes in this vignette. What are the key differences — not just physical, but emotional?

At the start of the book, the author describes the house on Mango Street in detail — the red bricks, the tight stairs, the small windows. How does Esperanza feel about that house? How does she feel about the imagined house in this vignette?

10. This vignette is written like a poem more than a story. How does the author's use of images — snow, paper, poetry — change the way the reader understands Esperanza's dream? Would the vignette be as powerful if it were written in plain, factual sentences?

Try rewriting the first sentence in plain language: "I want a house that belongs only to me." Now read the author's version. What does the author's language add that the plain version doesn't have?

Mango Says Goodbye Sometimes

Vignette 44

Part A — Multiple Choice

1. At the beginning of this vignette, what does Esperanza say she likes to do?

2. The author writes that Esperanza will leave Mango Street but will come back. Who does she say she is coming back for?

3. The author writes: "I make it mine even in my going away." What does "it" refer to?

4. The final vignette echoes the very first vignette, "The House on Mango Street." The author returns to the same place and the same house. What does this circular structure suggest?

5. The author has Esperanza say she will not always be here but "the house, the street, the town" will always be inside her. This connects to what the three sisters told her. What did the three sisters say that this echoes?

6. The title is "Mango Says Goodbye Sometimes." Why does the author say "sometimes" — not "always" or "forever"?

Part B — Open Response

7. The book begins and ends on Mango Street. Esperanza is ashamed of the street at the beginning. By the end, does she still feel only shame — or something more complicated? Use specific words from the final vignette to support your answer.

Look at what Esperanza says about leaving AND coming back. She says she "makes it mine." Is that shame? Is it pride? Or is it something else — acceptance? Responsibility? Find the words that show how she feels.

8. The three sisters told Esperanza to come back for those who cannot leave. In this final vignette, Esperanza says she will come back. But she says "they will not know I have gone away to come back." What does this mysterious line mean? How can coming back happen without people knowing you left?

Think about what Esperanza does — she writes stories. She tells stories of the people on Mango Street. Is writing a way of "coming back"? Can you come back through your writing even if you are physically far away?

9. The author has written a book about Mango Street from beginning to end. At the end, Esperanza says she likes to tell stories and then tells the story of the house on Mango Street. Is the author suggesting that Esperanza and the author are the same person? What does this moment reveal about what writing means in the book?

The book you've been reading IS the story of Mango Street. And now in the final vignette, Esperanza says she is going to tell "the story of the house on Mango Street." What does that mean about who is telling this story, and why?

10. You have now read all 44 vignettes of The House on Mango Street. Looking back at the whole book: what is the most important thing Esperanza learned about herself? Use evidence from at least two different vignettes — not just the final one — to support your answer.

Think about who Esperanza was at the beginning (ashamed, uncertain) and who she is at the end (claiming her story, her house, her responsibility). What changed? What moments in the book caused that change?
